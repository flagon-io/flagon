People expect this page to be a roadmap: a list of features with quarters next to them. It isn't, and it won't be. We keep a [public roadmap](/roadmap) for what we're building; this page is about the direction underneath it. Dated roadmaps age badly and mostly exist to make a company look decisive on a slide. What we can tell you honestly is the kind of company we're building toward, and how we'd know we were succeeding. That's more durable than a Gantt chart, and it's the part that actually steers the decisions.

## The company we're building toward

We're building a small software company that's still here in ten years, still making things people are glad to use, still run in the open. Not the biggest, not the loudest, not the one with the flashiest funding announcement. The one that's good, and stays good, and that people trust because it's earned it over a long time.

That's a specific ambition, not a modest one. Plenty of companies get big. Very few stay good while doing it. We'd rather be the second kind. This is what [the long game](/handbook/values#the-long-game) means in practice: we optimize for still being proud of this years from now, which rules out a lot of tempting shortcuts today.

## How we'd know we're winning

Growth charts are easy to game and easy to fake, so we don't treat them as the scoreboard. Here's what we actually watch.

**Craft that holds up.** The real test isn't whether the demo dazzles. It's whether the thing still feels good to use a year in, when the novelty's gone and it's just part of your day. Work that ages well is the hardest kind to fake, so it's the kind we measure ourselves against.

**Trust you can measure by reading us.** Because we work in the open, anyone can check whether we do what we say. If the handbook and the behavior match, if you can read our reasoning and find it honest, that's a form of winning that doesn't show up in revenue. We want to be a company you can audit and come away trusting more, not less.

**Durability.** Can we survive a bad quarter without abandoning our principles? Can we say no to money that would cost us the values? A company that only holds its line when times are good doesn't really have a line. Winning is being the kind of business that stays itself under pressure.

<Callout type="brand" title="The scoreboard we care about">
  Would the people who use our work be genuinely sad if we disappeared? Would
  the people who build it be proud to say they did? If both are yes, we're
  winning, whatever the chart says.
</Callout>

## People who are proud to work here

The other half of "where we're going" is who's coming with us. We want Flagon to be a place a small crew of capable people are genuinely proud to be part of, because the work is good, the treatment is fair, and nobody has to leave their integrity at the door.

That's a real constraint on how we grow. We'd rather stay small and be somewhere people are proud of than get big and become somewhere people tolerate. If growing means turning this into a place we wouldn't recommend to a friend, then growing that way is off the table.

## Direction, not a promise about products

Notice what this page doesn't do: it doesn't name a specific product, a date, or a feature. That's deliberate. What we make will change as we learn, and pinning ourselves to a wall of dated promises is exactly the kind of showmanship we try to avoid. See [built in public](/handbook/values) for why.

What won't change is the direction: good things, made honestly, for the long term, by people who are proud to make them. Everything specific serves that. If you want to hold us to something, hold us to this.
