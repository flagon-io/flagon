We're small. Small enough that we can't hide behind a diversity report or an affinity-group org chart, and we wouldn't want to. What we can do is say clearly what we believe and hold ourselves to it while the crew is still small enough that every hire moves the whole average. This page is that commitment, written down early so we can be caught not living up to it later.

## A mix of people makes the work better

This isn't charity and it isn't a quota. A room where everyone has the same background, the same schools, the same defaults, is a room with the same blind spots repeated in every chair. You ship the bug none of you could see because none of you have ever hit it. You write the empty state that makes perfect sense to people exactly like you and reads as hostile to everyone else.

Different lives produce different questions. Someone who has been the person a product wasn't built for asks better questions about who yours leaves out. That is not a soft benefit. It shows up in the code, the copy, the support queue, and the decisions we don't regret in three years. It's our [long-term thinking](/handbook/values) applied to who's in the room.

## Hire to widen the door, not tick a box

When we [hire](/handbook/how-we-hire), the goal is to widen the set of people who could plausibly get the job, not to backfill a demographic line. That means writing job posts that describe the work honestly instead of a wish list that only one narrow career path could satisfy. It means not requiring a pedigree as a proxy for skill. It means looking in more than one place, because the same three networks return the same three kinds of people.

Then we judge everyone by the same bar. Widening the door and lowering the bar are different things, and we do exactly one of them. A fair process that more people can enter beats a "diverse" hire made to feel good about a number, which is condescending to the person hired and useless to the work.

<Callout type="note" title="Why we don't wave numbers around">
  At our size, one hire swings every percentage wildly, so we don't wave numbers around as proof of anything. We'd rather point at the process and let you check whether it's fair.
</Callout>

## Belonging is a thing you build daily

Diversity is who's in the room. Belonging is whether they can do their best work once they're in it, and it isn't a poster or a Slack emoji. It's built or eroded in small moments: whose ideas get built on and whose get talked over, who gets the benefit of the doubt in a code review, who ends up doing the invisible glue work that never shows up in a promotion case.

So we pay attention to the small stuff. We give [feedback](/handbook/feedback) to everyone, because withholding the hard, useful note from someone is its own quiet exclusion. We write things down and default to public so that being in the right DM at the right time isn't the price of being included. We assume good faith and we say the real thing kindly.

## Paying fairly is part of this

You can't claim to value people and underpay the ones with the least leverage to argue. We tie belonging to money on purpose: our [compensation](/handbook/compensation) approach is meant to be legible and consistent, so pay tracks the work rather than who negotiated hardest or who a manager happened to like. Fair, boring, defensible pay is one of the least glamorous and most real diversity commitments a company can make.

We're at the start of this, not the end. As we grow, the test is whether these hold when they cost something. If you catch us falling short, that's a bug.
