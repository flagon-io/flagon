The brand is the accumulation of every ordinary interaction, not a page anyone reads before deciding to trust us. It is made or lost in the small, unglamorous surfaces far more than in a hero image. Here is how the same instincts apply where they actually meet people.

## The site

The site is often the first and last impression. Keep it honest and specific. Say what a thing does, in the present tense, without hype adjectives. Lead every page with the real point in the first paragraph. Use the schematic, technical visual system rather than stock photography. If a section exists only to fill space, cut it.

## Docs and release notes

These are read by people mid-problem, so clarity beats personality. Get to the answer fast, show the code, and be exact. A little dry wit is welcome in a release note; none is welcome in a paragraph someone is reading because something broke. Never dress up a limitation as a feature. If something does not work yet the way someone expects, say so and say what to do instead.

## Support

Support reaches someone at the exact moment their trust is up for grabs, which makes it the brand surface that matters most. The rules:

- **Answer like a person who can actually fix it,** because at Flagon the people answering can. No script, no "I'll pass this along" black hole.
- **Be specific, especially with bad news.** Instead of "I'll share your feedback," say why the thing works the way it does and what the person can do next.
- **Fix our own mistakes generously.** If someone gets a surprise bill because our pricing or a default confused them, we make it right. Being right is worth more than the revenue.

## Social and public replies

Every public reply is the brand talking. Be helpful, be human, and do not get defensive when someone is critical, even when they are wrong. If they are being genuinely unreasonable, the move is to be the calmer, bigger party, or to step away. Wit is fine. Punching down, sarcasm at a confused person, and corporate defensiveness are not.

## Swag and physical things

Merch is a thank-you, not an ad. Give it to people who say kind things or show up and help, and make it something they would actually wear or use. A good sticker earns more goodwill than a campaign. A cheap, ugly one costs more than it looks like it should, because it is the brand in someone's hands.

## When something new comes up

New surfaces will keep appearing that none of these pages predicted. The answer is always the same two questions from [the foundations](/handbook/brand-foundations): would this hold up to a skeptical builder looking closely, and would we be happy to be treated this way? If yes to both, ship it. If not, you already know what to change.
