We deleted one month of events from the book's `events` table, 164,383 rows, with an ordinary `delete ... where created_at ...`. It wrote 32 MB of WAL, left 164,383 [dead tuples](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#dead-tuple) behind, and the vacuum that cleaned them up wrote another 12 MB. Then we removed the same month from a partitioned copy of the table by detaching and dropping one partition. That wrote under 150 KB.

<Fig name="detach-vs-delete">

**Deleting a month is work; dropping one is a file.** The delete left 164,383 dead tuples and wrote 32 MB of WAL, and the vacuum after it wrote another 12 MB. Detaching and dropping the month's partition unlinks its files and wrote under 150 KB.

</Fig>

At two billion rows, that `delete` becomes the nightly job that runs for hours and floods the replicas. The drop stays the same size: unlink some files, update the catalog.

That's the honest pitch. Partitioning is a data lifecycle feature first. It isn't a general speedup, and for queries that don't carry the partition key it makes things slower.

## Every benefit comes from separate tables

A partitioned table is a parent that holds no rows. Every row lives in exactly one child, a **partition**, chosen by the value of the **partition key**. Each partition is an ordinary table with its own heap, indexes, visibility map, and autovacuum schedule. That separation buys four things:

- **Retention is a drop.** Removing old data is dropping a table: no dead tuples, almost no WAL, nothing for vacuum.
- **Maintenance works on pieces.** Autovacuum handles a 30 GB partition, not a 3 TB table. Old partitions freeze once, and later vacuums skip their pages. You can reindex or repack one at a time.
- **Hot indexes stay small.** The current month's indexes fit in memory even when the full history doesn't.
- **Pruning.** A query that filters on the key touches only the partitions that can match.

## Partitioning doesn't make lookups faster

A B-tree descent costs three or four pages at a million rows or a billion ([A B-tree is a shallow tree of pages](/books/eight-kilobytes/btree#a-b-tree-is-a-shallow-tree-of-pages)), so splitting a table doesn't make a well-indexed lookup faster. When the query doesn't name the key, splitting multiplies the lookups: a lookup by `id` read 7 pages on `events` and 39 on `events_p`, its monthly-partitioned copy, searching every partition's index. With 14 partitions that's small. With 1,000 it isn't ([Every partition a query can't prune costs planning time](#every-partition-a-query-cant-prune-costs-planning-time)).

For faster time-range reads there's a cheaper tool: a 24 KB BRIN index on `created_at` ([BRIN keeps one summary per range of pages](/books/eight-kilobytes/more-indexes#brin-keeps-one-summary-per-range-of-pages)) counted March's events in 3,251 pages; the partitioned table, pruned to March, in 2,988.

So partition for what only partitioning gives you: cheap retention, per-partition maintenance, or a table whose vacuum and index builds no longer fit your windows. Leave a table under tens of gigabytes alone unless it has a retention requirement, and wait until `pg_stat_statements` confirms your top queries carry the key.

## Range fits time; list and hash have narrow jobs

Declarative partitioning (`partition by`, PostgreSQL 10+, better in every release since) has three strategies:

| Strategy | Key | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Range | a continuous value, usually time | events, logs, metrics, anything with retention |
| List | discrete values | regions, a few very large tenants |
| Hash | hash of the key, modulo N | splitting one huge hot table into equal pieces |

This chapter's `events_p` is range-partitioned by month: fourteen partitions of about 3,000 pages each, against 35,173 for `events`. Its primary key is `(id, created_at)`, which is required ([why](#unique-constraints-must-include-the-key)).

List fits few, stable values, like EU data on its own storage. Hash gives no lifecycle benefit; it only splits one huge, busy table into medium ones for maintenance.

## Choose the key you delete by

The partition key should be what you retire data by, and ideally what most queries filter on. For events, logs, and metrics those are the same column, the timestamp, which is why they partition so well.

When they differ, retention usually wins. A SaaS app filters by `account_id`, but partitioning by account makes retention a row-by-row delete again. Partition by time and lead each partition's indexes with `account_id`; spreading tenants across machines is [sharding](/books/eight-kilobytes/sharding)'s job.

Let retention pick the granularity, and aim for partitions of several gigabytes and counts in the tens to low hundreds. Monthly for ten years is 120 partitions. Daily is 3,650, and you'll feel it.

## The bounds are a free, coarse index

**Partition pruning** is skipping the partitions that can't hold matching rows. Counting March's events, with no index on `created_at`, read 2,988 pages on `events_p` against 35,173 on `events`: only March's partition even appears in the plan.

When the value isn't known at plan time (`now()`, a [generic plan](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#generic-plan)'s parameter), Postgres prunes as execution starts, and the plan says `Subplans Removed: 11`. Either way it needs the bare key: `date_trunc('day', created_at) = ...` scans all fourteen partitions, the half-open range scans one ([Keep the indexed column bare](/books/eight-kilobytes/queries#keep-the-indexed-column-bare)).

Without the key, even a cheap query pays at planning time: "account 42's latest 20 events" read partitions newest first and stopped at 23 pages, like the plain table, but planning touched 455 pages against 54.

## Partition-wise plans are for reports

Pruning works one table at a time. `enable_partitionwise_join` joins two identically partitioned tables partition by partition, and `enable_partitionwise_aggregate` groups per partition. Both are off by default because every per-partition hash or sort gets its own [`work_mem`](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#work_mem): a plan over 100 partitions can use 100 times your budget.

On a quarterly join of `events_p` to a `deliveries_p` partitioned by the same months, it cut 46.9 ms to 17.6. But stating the range on both sides, so each side prunes alone, was the simpler fix. Use the settings per session for reports, never for OLTP.

## Constraints must carry the partition key

### Unique constraints must include the key

Here's why `events_p`'s primary key is `(id, created_at)`:

```text
ERROR:  unique constraint on partitioned table must include all partitioning columns
DETAIL:  UNIQUE constraint on table "events_p" lacks column "created_at"
which is part of the partition key.
```

Postgres has no global index. An index on a partitioned table is one index per partition, so uniqueness is checked within one partition. If the unique key contains the partition key, equal keys land together; if not, duplicates can sit in different months unseen. (The same `id` went into May and September without complaint.) `on conflict` targets follow the same rule.

### References into a partitioned table must carry the key

Every unique key on `events_p` includes `created_at`, so `references events_p (id)` fails, and a referencing table must carry the partition key too, as `deliveries_p` does with `(event_id, event_created_at)`. Event tables partition well partly because few tables reference them.

<Callout type="danger" title="Decide which guarantees you give up before you partition">
List every unique constraint and foreign key that touches the table before you start, and decide what replaces each: include the key, keep a small unpartitioned table for the unique value, or don't partition.
</Callout>

Indexes have their own catch: `create index concurrently` isn't supported on a partitioned parent. Create the index `on only` the parent, build each partition's concurrently, and attach each; the parent index turns valid after the last.

The duplicate needs something to supply ids explicitly (imports, `overriding system value`); an identity column hands out unique values on its own.

The index build, generated per partition:

```sql
create index events_p_kind_idx on only events_p (kind);   -- indisvalid = f

select format('create index concurrently %I on %s (kind)',
              c.relname || '_kind_idx', c.oid::regclass),
       format('alter index events_p_kind_idx attach partition %I',
              c.relname || '_kind_idx')
from pg_inherits i join pg_class c on c.oid = i.inhrelid
where i.inhparent = 'events_p'::regclass
order by c.relname
\gexec
```

`create index concurrently` can't run inside a transaction block, so run the statements one at a time (psql's `\gexec` does, outside `begin`). If one build fails it leaves an invalid index on that partition; drop it and build again ([When a concurrent build fails](/books/eight-kilobytes/migrations#when-a-concurrent-build-fails)).

</DeepDive>

## Avoid the default partition

A default partition catches rows that match no other. It looks like a safety net. It's closer to a trap: creating a partition must scan it under lock and fails if a stray row for that range is there, it blocks `detach ... concurrently`, and with a foreign key pointing at the table you can't drop it directly.

Make sure future partitions always exist instead, and treat a "no partition found" error as the alarm it should be.

<DeepDive title="The three errors a default partition earns you">

DETAIL:  constraint deliveries_p_event_id_event_created_at_fkey on table deliveries_p
depends on table events_p_default

(We emptied it, then `detach partition events_p_default` followed by `drop table` worked.)

## Retention: detach, then drop

This is the payoff. Deleting November 2025 (164,383 rows) two ways:

| | `delete` + vacuum | detach + drop |
|---|---|---|
| WAL written | 32.5 MB + 12.5 MB | about 140 KB |
| WAL records | 175,000+ | 226 |
| Dead tuples for vacuum | 164,383 | none |
| Index cleanup | every index, every row | none |
| Grows with | rows deleted | number of indexes |

The `delete` took under 100 ms, on small, cached data. Its cost grows with the rows, and so does the WAL every replica replays. The drop's doesn't. Both finished in well under a second here; only one will at a billion rows.

```sql
alter table events_p detach partition events_p_2025_11 concurrently;
drop table events_p_2025_11;
```

### Detach concurrently, or everything queues behind you

A plain `detach` takes an `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` lock on the parent and queues everything behind it ([the lock queue](/books/eight-kilobytes/concurrency#a-waiting-lock-blocks-everyone-behind-it)). `detach partition ... concurrently` (PostgreSQL 14+) doesn't block queries. It can't run in a transaction block, and if interrupted it leaves the partition "detach pending" until `detach partition ... finalize`.

### Foreign keys set the order

With `deliveries_p` referencing `events_p`, detaching the events partition first fails with a foreign key violation. Retire the referencing partition first.

<Callout type="tip" title="A retention job that won't page you">
Daily, in order: create missing future partitions, finalize pending detaches, detach expired partitions concurrently (children first), archive if needed, drop, `analyze` the parent. Set `lock_timeout` so it gives up instead of queueing, and let the next run retry.
</Callout>

## Create partitions before you need them

Retention removes partitions at one end; something has to add them at the other. A missing partition doesn't degrade gracefully: every insert for that range fails. Keep several future partitions ready, created by a scheduled job, and alert when the furthest-ahead one is less than a few weeks out. The `\gexec` generator is most of that job. If you'd rather not own it, `pg_partman` is the extension most teams use ([pg_cron and pg_partman keep time on your side](/books/eight-kilobytes/extensions#pg_cron-and-pg_partman-keep-time-on-your-side)); read its defaults for your version, because they matter.

<DeepDive title="What pg_partman does, and the default it creates">

- Version 5 manages only native declarative partitioning; the older trigger-based method was removed.
- `create_parent()` registers an existing partitioned table with a control column and an interval, and creates partitions ahead of time (`p_premake` defaults to 4).
- `run_maintenance()` (or `run_maintenance_proc()`, which commits after each partition set) creates new partitions and applies retention. Run it from its background worker or a scheduler like `pg_cron`.
- Retention lives in its `part_config` table: `retention` sets the age, and `retention_keep_table` (default true) detaches old partitions and keeps them rather than dropping them.
- `create_parent()` creates a default partition unless you pass `p_default_table := false`. Given the problems above, consider turning it off, or at least monitor it for rows.

</DeepDive>

## Convert a live table without downtime

You can't `alter table ... partition by`. Instead, make the whole existing table the first partition, covering all of history, and let new partitions take the future. The trick is a `check` constraint that proves every row fits, so `attach partition` skips its validation scan:

1. **Online:** build a unique index on `(id, created_at)` concurrently, and add `check (created_at < '2026-11-01')` as `not valid`, then `validate` it without blocking writes ([the two-step move](/books/eight-kilobytes/migrations#add-a-check-not-valid-then-validate-it)).
2. **One short transaction, with `lock_timeout`:** rename the old table, make that index its primary key, move the identity column to a new partitioned parent, attach the old table up to the bound, create the next partitions, and `setval` the parent's sequence past the old ids. A partition can't keep its own identity, so skipping that step fails the attach.

On a 1.8 million row copy the transaction took about 20 ms. Without the constraint, the attach scanned every row under its lock: minutes or hours on a terabyte. (Filling new partitions in bulk: [Freeze the partition, attach it built, analyze the parent](/books/eight-kilobytes/loading#freeze-the-partition-attach-it-built-analyze-the-parent).)

<DeepDive title="The conversion, statement by statement">

```sql
create unique index concurrently events_copy_id_created_at_key
  on events_copy (id, created_at);

alter table events_copy add constraint events_copy_old_range
  check (created_at < '2026-11-01') not valid;
alter table events_copy validate constraint events_copy_old_range;
```

Our first attach failed twice, with `The new partition may not contain an identity column.` and then `multiple primary keys for table "events_copy_old" are not allowed`. The old primary key on `(id)` has to go, the step 1 index becomes the new one (which `attach` adopts instead of building an index under lock), and the identity moves to the parent:

```sql
begin;
set local lock_timeout = '2s';

alter table events_copy rename to events_copy_old;
alter table events_copy_old drop constraint events_copy_pkey;
alter table events_copy_old
  add constraint events_copy_old_pkey
  primary key using index events_copy_id_created_at_key;
alter table events_copy_old alter column id drop identity;

create table events_copy (
  id          bigint generated always as identity,
  account_id  bigint not null,
  project_id  bigint not null,
  user_id     bigint,
  kind        text not null,
  payload     jsonb not null default '{}',
  created_at  timestamptz not null default now(),
  primary key (id, created_at)
) partition by range (created_at);

alter table events_copy attach partition events_copy_old
  for values from (minvalue) to ('2026-11-01');

create table events_copy_2026_11 partition of events_copy
  for values from ('2026-11-01') to ('2026-12-01');
create table events_copy_2026_12 partition of events_copy
  for values from ('2026-12-01') to ('2027-01-01');

select setval(pg_get_serial_sequence('events_copy', 'id'),
              (select max(id) from events_copy_old));
commit;
```

With `client_min_messages = debug1`, the attach says `partition constraint for table "events_copy_old" is implied by existing constraints`, in 0.468 ms. Afterwards an insert of one row for now and one for 2026-11-15 got ids 2000001 and 2000002, in `events_copy_old` and `events_copy_2026_11`.

The old partition holds all of history, and retention on it stays a `delete` until its range expires (or you split months out of it in the background). Before trying this for real, check foreign keys that reference the old table, triggers and grants (they don't move by themselves), and code that names the sequence.

## Partitioning costs most when queries can't prune

### Every partition a query can't prune costs planning time

A query that prunes barely notices how many partitions there are. One that can't pays for each, in planning time, memory, and locks. We planned a query with the key and one without (`where account_id = 42`) on 12 to 3,000 daily partitions:

| Partitions | With key, warm | Without key, warm | Without key, cold | Planner memory, without key |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 0.02 ms | 0.05 ms | 0.9 ms | 124 KB |
| 365 | 0.13 ms | 4.2 ms | 19 ms | 3.1 MB |
| 1,000 | 0.13 ms | 15.0 ms | 55 ms | 8.4 MB |
| 3,000 | 0.14 ms | 52.0 ms | 170 ms | 27 MB |

With the key, the count barely matters. Without it, planning takes 52 ms, and the first query on every new connection about three times that. Unpruned queries also lock every partition and index, spilling into the shared lock table until it can run out with "out of shared memory". Keep counts in the tens to low hundreds, and make the hot queries prune.

<DeepDive title="How we measured planning, and the locks it takes">

Warm numbers are medians of 15 runs in one session; cold is the median of five first plans, each in a new session. Each table had a primary key and an index on `account_id`. Planner memory is from `explain (memory)`, PostgreSQL 17+. The lab re-runs the first three rows.

Locks held by `select count(*) from pt_N where account_id = 42` inside a transaction:

| Partitions | Fast-path locks | Locks in the shared lock table |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 40 | 0 |
| 365 | 64 | 1,035 |
| 1,000 | 64 | 2,940 |
| 3,000 | 64 | 8,940 |

Each backend has a few fast-path lock slots that avoid shared memory. In PostgreSQL 18 their number scales with `max_locks_per_transaction` (64 here); earlier versions had a fixed 16. The rest go into the shared lock table (`LWLock LockManager` waits under concurrency), sized at roughly `max_locks_per_transaction` times the number of connections.

### Four smaller traps catch partitioned tables

- **Lookups by id alone** search every partition. Put the time in the URL, or use an id that encodes it ([UUIDv7](/books/eight-kilobytes/keys#uuidv7-is-the-default-and-it-reveals-creation-time) does).
- **The parent is never analyzed** by autovacuum. Analyze it from the retention job.
- **Updates that change the key** move the row: a delete plus an insert, never [HOT](/books/eight-kilobytes/hot). Pick a key that doesn't change.
- **Partitioning too small.** A 5 GB table in 365 daily partitions gets all the overhead and none of the benefit.

## The short version

- Partition for lifecycle. Deleting a month cost 45 MB of WAL and 164,383 dead tuples; dropping its partition, under 150 KB.
- It's not an OLTP speedup: queries without the key search every partition, and BRIN gives most of the time-range benefit.
- Partition by the key you delete by (usually time); keep counts in the tens to low hundreds.
- Compare the bare key with a half-open range, and check pruning in `EXPLAIN`.
- Every unique key must include the partition key, and references must carry it.
- Retire with `detach ... concurrently` and `drop`, children first, with `lock_timeout`. Avoid default partitions.
- Create future partitions ahead of time, and alert when they run short.
- Convert a live table by attaching it whole, with a validated `check` constraint so the attach skips its scan.
