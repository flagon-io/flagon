Two providers, same 4 vCPU and 32 GB of RAM, same benchmark. One vendor's published run puts itself at 18,000 queries per second and its rival at 12,000. Take the numbers at face value and they still don't say either one runs a better PostgreSQL. One keeps its pages on a drive inside the machine, the other on storage across the network, and the dataset is fifteen times the size of RAM.

That's most of the provider landscape. Above the buffer cache, everyone runs the same engine. Below it and around it, they bet differently: where pages live, who holds the superuser, what you pay for, and how hard it is to leave.

Decide how much management to buy before you decide whose; [Buy as much management as you can afford](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#buy-as-much-management-as-you-can-afford) makes that case. Then our advice is short: pick for the workload you have, and pick something you can leave.

## Know the shape and you know the tradeoffs

Every option is one of five architectures, or a blend, and the shape tells you most of the tradeoffs before you read a feature list.

| Shape | Where the pages live | Good at | Costs you | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classic managed | VM plus network volume | Familiar, portable | A miss is a network trip | RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure |
| Local NVMe | Drive in the machine | Fast past RAM | Replicas carry durability | PlanetScale Metal |
| Disaggregated | Storage fed by WAL | Shared replicas, auto-growth | Proprietary storage | Aurora, AlloyDB |
| Serverless | Disaggregated, scales to zero | Cheap idle, branches | Cold starts, busy bills | Neon |
| Platform bundle | Any of the above | Auth, APIs, storage | More of your app on one vendor | Supabase |

Most specialists are classic managed too.

Self-managing, on VMs or Kubernetes, is the sixth option, and it can take any of the first three shapes.

## Storage decides the benchmark

When one provider posts twice the throughput of another on the same vCPU and RAM, look at the disk first. Once the [working set](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#working-set) no longer fits in memory, where the pages live sets the speed.

The [latency chapter](/books/eight-kilobytes/latency#memory-is-cheap-everything-else-is-expensive) has the ladder: RAM in nanoseconds, local NVMe in tens of microseconds, network storage in hundreds of microseconds or more. And the ladder only matters for misses ([shared_buffers is an array of pages](/books/eight-kilobytes/buffers#shared_buffers-is-an-array-of-pages)). If your hot pages fit in memory, all three storage designs look alike.

### Network block storage: every miss is a round trip

RDS, Cloud SQL, Azure, and most specialists put the data directory on a network volume, so a read that misses memory becomes a request to storage servers elsewhere in the data center. The volume has an [IOPS](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#iops) ceiling, and the instance in front of it often has a lower one: an EC2 `r8g.xlarge` (4 vCPU, 32 GiB) sustains 6,000 IOPS once its burst is spent, whatever volume you bought ([Two ceilings, and you hit the lower one](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#two-ceilings-and-you-hit-the-lower-one)).

It earns its place as the default anyway. The volume outlives the instance, so a dead host means reattaching, not rebuilding. Snapshots are cheap, storage and compute resize independently, and even a single instance survives a dead disk, because the storage replicates underneath it (not forever: AWS rates gp3 at 99.8 to 99.9 percent durability a year).

### Local NVMe: fast, and gone with the host

Instance storage is a drive in the same machine. AWS rates an `i8g.xlarge`, the same 4 vCPU and 32 GiB, at 150,000 random-read IOPS on its one 937 GB drive: twenty-five times the network path's 6,000.

The price is durability. AWS: "the data does not persist if the instance is stopped, hibernated, or terminated". So a database on local NVMe gets its durability from [replication](/books/eight-kilobytes/replication), and that has consequences. A commit must reach a replica in another zone before it's safe, which [costs a round trip](/books/eight-kilobytes/replication#synchronous-commit-costs-a-round-trip). A lost node is rebuilt by copying the whole database, hours for terabytes. And disk size is instance size: outgrowing the drive means migrating to a bigger node.

<DeepDive title="The volume and instance numbers, as AWS and Google publish them">

As of October 2026, per AWS:

- **gp3** includes a 3,000 IOPS baseline ([the managed chapter](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#the-volume-is-your-bottleneck-now) shows what that ceiling looks like), can be provisioned up to 80,000 IOPS and 2,000 MiB/s, and does not burst ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ebs/latest/userguide/general-purpose.html)). - **io2 Block Express** is "designed to deliver an average latency of under 500 microseconds for 16KiB I/O operations", reaches 256,000 IOPS on Nitro instances, and is rated "99.999% durability", priced per provisioned IOPS.
- **`r8g.xlarge`** has a baseline of 6,000 IOPS and 156.25 MB/s and a maximum of 40,000 IOPS and 1,250 MB/s; instances in its tier "can sustain the maximum performance for 30 minutes at least once every 24 hours, after which they revert to their baseline performance" ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/ebs-optimized.html)).
- **`i8g.xlarge`** is rated at 150,000 random-read and 82,500 write IOPS, at 4 KiB and full queue depth ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ec2/latest/instancetypes/so.html)). ### Log-structured storage: ship WAL, not pages

Aurora, AlloyDB, Neon, and Azure's HorizonDB (in preview) send WAL to a storage service that builds pages from it, so a write waits on a network quorum and a read miss is a network fetch, softened by a local cache. What that changes for tuning is in [Disaggregated storage changes some rules](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#disaggregated-storage-changes-some-rules). For choosing, the difference is the bill: Aurora Standard charges per million I/O requests, AlloyDB has no I/O charges, and Neon's local cache can spill to NVMe.

### The best-known benchmark tested storage at fifteen times RAM

The best-known comparison is PlanetScale's, published July 1, 2025 with its Postgres launch and still on its [benchmarks page](https://planetscale.com/benchmarks) as of October 2026. On a TPCC-like test with a roughly 500 GB database on 32 GB machines, it reports itself, on local NVMe, at about 18,000 QPS, and Aurora at about 12,000. All of it is vendor-reported; we have not reproduced it.

The architecture explains the shape. With the dataset fifteen times RAM, most reads miss memory: a local read on NVMe, a network round trip under an IOPS policy everywhere else. PlanetScale pays the network on every commit instead, waiting for a replica in another zone, and saves it on every read miss. The rivals' replies confirm the physics: put the hot pages on local flash or in RAM, and the network penalty shrinks.

<DeepDive title="The PlanetScale benchmark, its method, and the replies">

Per its [methodology post](https://planetscale.com/blog/benchmarking-postgres):

- **System under test:** PlanetScale's `M-320`, an `i8g` node (4 vCPU, 32 GB RAM, 937 GB local NVMe), as a primary and two replicas across three zones; only the primary served the benchmark.
- **The others:** single instances matching or exceeding that vCPU and RAM, such as Aurora on `db.r8g.xlarge` and Neon at 8 compute units, all of which "use network-attached storage".
- **Workloads:** Percona's TPCC-like sysbench script at a scale that "produces a ~500 gigabyte Postgres database", 5 minutes at 32 and 64 connections; sysbench read-only on "a ~300 gigabyte" dataset; and `SELECT 1` repeated for round-trip latency. Settings "left at each platform's defaults" except connection limits and timeouts; cost compared as its three-node price against each rival with replicas added.

Headline TPCC-like results, as PlanetScale states them: about 18,000 QPS for itself, Aurora about 12,000 ([Aurora page](https://planetscale.com/benchmarks/aurora)), Neon about 12,500 ([Neon page](https://planetscale.com/benchmarks/neon-lakebase)), and lower figures for AlloyDB, Crunchy Data, Supabase, TigerData, and Heroku. Read-only: PlanetScale and Aurora both "around ~35,000 QPS", PlanetScale steadier at p99. Its [architecture page](https://planetscale.com/docs/postgres/postgres-architecture) says changes are "confirmed by at least one replica".

- **Xata** ([July 16, 2025](https://xata.io/blog/reaction-to-the-planetscale-postgresql-benchmarks)) ran the test on its own NVMe-over-Fabrics network storage and reported about 25,000 QPS at 64 connections on 8 vCPU with Postgres tuned for the run, and about 20,000 on 4 vCPU. It calls its chart "not an apples-to-apples comparison", and faulted the method: no checkpoint or vacuum before the run, untuned defaults, a "very write heavy" workload. PlanetScale [replied](https://planetscale.com/benchmarks/xata) that Xata tuned for the workload and declined to give PlanetScale access to reproduce it.
- **Neon** ([July 11, 2025](https://neon.com/blog/separation-of-storage-and-compute-perf)) reran the test with a 500 GB local cache on NVMe and reported 17,030 QPS against 12,512 at its defaults, and about 10 percent less peak QPS with a 180 GB cache sized to the working set. Its counterpoint: fixed local disks make you buy the next size up.

</DeepDive>

### Ask who ran a benchmark before you read its numbers

These questions apply to every vendor benchmark, including the ones that flatter a service you like:

- **Who ran it?** A vendor tunes its own product and tests rivals' defaults; "vendor-reported" belongs next to every number.
- **Which workload?** Percona's README says its script "is NOT an implementation of TPCC workload" and asks that vendor comparisons say so ([Percona](https://github.com/Percona-Lab/sysbench-tpcc)).
- **Dataset against RAM?** A dataset 15 times RAM tests storage. Production is usually sized so the working set fits in memory.
- **Same durability, same tuning?** Synchronous replicas, quorum storage, and one volume promise different things, and [provider defaults differ widely](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#the-defaults-are-not-the-books-defaults).
- **Normalized and reproducible?** Price per query depends on tier, region, and replica count; published scripts make a claim checkable.

A day with your schema and your ten heaviest queries on two candidate services tells you more than every published chart ([how to run it](/books/eight-kilobytes/benchmarking)).

### Prove the disk is the problem before you move

A benchmark can tell you a faster disk exists. Only your own numbers can tell you that you need one. Compare the working set to RAM ([size RAM to the working set](/books/eight-kilobytes/latency#size-ram-to-the-working-set)). [Sample wait events](/books/eight-kilobytes/observability#sample-waits-to-see-where-the-time-goes): sessions mostly in `IO` / `DataFileRead` mean reads are leaving memory. Rank `pg_stat_statements` by `shared_blks_read` ([four rankings](/books/eight-kilobytes/observability#rank-the-same-view-four-ways-total-time-first)); often one query reads most of the pages. And look for a cap: IOPS flat while latency climbs ([how to tell](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#throttled-io-shows-as-flat-iops-and-rising-latency)).

Then try the cheap fixes in order: fewer pages read, more RAM, provisioned IOPS, a bigger instance ([Earn the bigger box](/books/eight-kilobytes/scale#earn-the-bigger-box)). Move architectures only when storage reads still dominate after all that.

## Hyperscalers win on breadth and cost you complexity

Each big cloud sells a classic managed service and a disaggregated one. They win on breadth: regions, compliance programs, private networking to everything else you run there. They cost you complexity: bills with many line items, and paid extended support if you fall behind on major versions.

As of October 2026:

## Specialists buy you closer support and fewer regions

These companies sell Postgres and little else. Our view: that focus buys Postgres-specific features and closer support, at the cost of fewer regions and a smaller company behind the service.

Read the HA and backup lines closely; they vary far more here than among the hyperscalers, from 2 days of [PITR](/books/eight-kilobytes/glossary#pitr) to 30, and from hot standbys to asynchronous ones that can lose the last commits. Of those that say, only Crunchy Bridge gives you superuser.

## Serverless platforms optimize for the developer's day

Their pitch: databases that cost nothing when idle, branches per pull request, and sometimes a whole backend around the database. The tradeoffs are cold starts, unit-based bills that are harder to forecast, and, for bundles, more of your app tied to one vendor.

<DeepDive title="Neon, Supabase, Xata, Nile, and Railway, entry by entry">

As of October 2026:

**Neon.** Compute and storage linked by "a stream of write-ahead log (WAL) records", now branded Lakebase Postgres ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/architecture-overview)); Databricks announced its intent to acquire Neon on May 14, 2025, and Neon "is part of the Databricks Platform" ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/databricks-agrees-acquire-neon-help-developers-deliver-ai-systems)). 14 to 18 ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/postgresql/postgres-version-policy)). Scales to zero after 5 idle minutes, waking "within a few hundred milliseconds" ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/scale-to-zero)); branches "from a current or past state" ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/branching)); restore windows up to 30 days by plan. A major upgrade means a new project; a connected logical replication subscriber keeps compute awake, and inactive slots go after about 40 hours ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/guides/logical-replication-neon)). Billed by compute-unit hours, with a free plan ([Neon](https://neon.com/docs/introduction/plans)).

**Supabase.** A platform bundle on a Postgres instance per project: Auth, a REST API via PostgREST, Realtime, Storage, and Edge Functions ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/getting-started/architecture)), on PostgreSQL 15 or 17 ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/upgrading)). Built on [row-level security](/books/eight-kilobytes/security), with documented logical replication out ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/setup-replication-external)). Branches start "Data-less by default" ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/deployment/branching)); PITR is an add-on needing at least Small compute; OrioleDB, a storage engine without vacuum, "is in public beta" ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/orioledb)). Plan plus compute plus usage ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/pricing)).

**Xata.** "Branch-native Postgres": each branch is "a Copy-on-Write snapshot of the underlying logical volume" ([Xata](https://xata.io/docs/core-concepts/branching)), with anonymized clones; 14 to 18 ([Xata](https://xata.io/docs/platform/extensions)); in Xata's cloud, yours, or self-hosted ([Xata](https://xata.io/pricing)). Only minor upgrades in place; Xata's public docs don't describe backups or PITR.

**Nile.** Postgres for multi-tenant SaaS: each storage page belongs to one tenant, and tenants go on shared or dedicated compute ([Nile](https://www.thenile.dev/docs/getting-started/architecture)), what the [sharding chapter](/books/eight-kilobytes/sharding) does by hand. PostgreSQL 15 only, and no user-defined functions, triggers, or sequences on tenant tables ([Nile](https://www.thenile.dev/docs/postgres/postgres-compatibility)).

**Railway.** Not managed: its templates are "unmanaged services", leaving you "responsible for" backups, tuning, security, and monitoring ([Railway](https://docs.railway.com/databases)). Optional Patroni HA ([Railway](https://docs.railway.com/databases/postgresql-ha)); backups are volume snapshots, not WAL-based recovery ([Railway](https://docs.railway.com/volumes/backups)).

Smaller entries exist too, such as Prisma Postgres, billed per operation ([Prisma](https://www.prisma.io/docs/postgres/faq)).

</DeepDive>

## Self-managing gives you the superuser and the pager

Self-managing on VMs or Kubernetes gives you the superuser, every extension, any version on release day, and the cheapest path at large, steady scale. It costs you the on-call rotation, and everything in the [replication](/books/eight-kilobytes/replication#dont-hand-roll-failover) and [backups](/books/eight-kilobytes/backups#test-your-restores) chapters becomes yours. The standard parts are Patroni for HA, pgBackRest, WAL-G, or Barman for backups, and an operator such as CloudNativePG on Kubernetes.

<DeepDive title="The self-managed toolkit, with licenses">

As of October 2026:

- **Patroni**: "a Python template for building PostgreSQL high availability (HA) clusters", with etcd, Consul, ZooKeeper, or Kubernetes for leader election; MIT, PostgreSQL 9.3 to 18 ([GitHub](https://github.com/patroni/patroni)).
- **pgBackRest**: full, differential, and incremental backups to S3, Azure, or GCS-compatible storage, with PITR ([pgbackrest.org](https://pgbackrest.org/)). Its maintainer announced on April 27, 2026 that it was "no longer being maintained", then on May 18 that "a coalition of sponsors has come together to fund ongoing development" ([news](https://pgbackrest.org/news.html)).
- **WAL-G** (Apache-2.0) and **Barman** (GPL-3.0) ([WAL-G](https://github.com/wal-g/wal-g), [Barman](https://pgbarman.org/)).
- **CloudNativePG**: an operator that "integrates directly with the Kubernetes API" instead of using Patroni; Apache-2.0, a CNCF Sandbox project since January 2025, backups through its Barman Cloud plugin ([GitHub](https://github.com/cloudnative-pg/cloudnative-pg), [CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/projects/cloudnativepg/)).
- **Crunchy Postgres for Kubernetes (PGO)**: Patroni plus pgBackRest, Apache-2.0; its default images fall under the Crunchy Data Developer Program terms, so read those first ([GitHub](https://github.com/CrunchyData/postgres-operator)).
- **Zalando postgres-operator**: Patroni via its Spilo image, MIT, PostgreSQL 14 to 18 ([GitHub](https://github.com/zalando/postgres-operator)).
- **StackGres**: AGPL-3.0, Patroni-based, with a web console and a large extension catalog ([StackGres](https://stackgres.io/)). **Percona Operator for PostgreSQL** is "based on Postgres Operator developed by Crunchy Data", Apache-2.0 ([GitHub](https://github.com/percona/percona-postgresql-operator)).

</DeepDive>

## Choose by elimination

Work through these in order. The early answers rule out most of the list.

1. **How big, and how fast is it growing?** Check the storage ceiling (1 TB on Fly.io Managed Postgres, 64 TiB on RDS and Azure, 256 TiB on Aurora), whether disk is tied to instance size, and your working set against affordable RAM.
2. **How much do you write?** Writes care about commit latency and WAL volume; reads about replicas and cache misses.
3. **Superuser or a specific extension?** Check the exact version and [footnotes](/books/eight-kilobytes/managed#extensions-come-from-an-allowlist). Superuser narrows the list to Crunchy Bridge, your own containers, or self-managed.
4. **What compliance?** Certifications and data residency differ per provider and region. Ask for the reports, not the logo wall.
5. **Which regions?** The app's region. Cross-region round trips undo everything else in this book ([round trips cost more than queries](/books/eight-kilobytes/latency#round-trips-cost-more-than-queries)).
6. **What RPO and RTO?** [Write them down first](/books/eight-kilobytes/backups#write-down-rpo-and-rto-first): asynchronous HA can lose recent commits, and a 2-day PITR window won't cover a bug found on day 3.
7. **Which budget model?** Fixed instances are predictable; usage-based compute is cheap idle and surprising busy; I/O-priced storage bills you for a missing index.
8. **How do you leave?** Answer the next section before you sign.

Our opinion: most teams starting today do well with a classic managed service on the cloud their app already runs on, sized so the working set fits in RAM. Reach for local NVMe, disaggregated storage, or serverless when you can name the problem they solve for you.

## Plan the exit before you need it

Every provider makes leaving possible. Few make it easy. The cost depends on how much of your system speaks something other than PostgreSQL.

### Community Postgres moves cleanly

Your schema, data, roles (mostly), and SQL come with you, as long as they stay on community PostgreSQL and community extensions. Two paths carry them.

**`pg_dump`** ([pg_dump](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/app-pgdump.html)) works everywhere, but the database is read-only or diverging for the hours it takes ([logical backups](/books/eight-kilobytes/backups#pg_dump-is-portable-small-and-slow-to-restore)), and roles may need scripting, since `pg_dumpall` needs superuser.

**Logical replication** then a cutover ([logical replication](/books/eight-kilobytes/replication#logical-replication-copies-rows-not-pages)) takes minutes of downtime instead of hours, if the old provider lets you publish. RDS, Aurora, Cloud SQL, AlloyDB (via pglogical), Azure, Neon, Supabase, and PlanetScale document it; mind its [limits](/books/eight-kilobytes/replication#logical-replication-skips-ddl-and-sequences).

### Proprietary storage, features, and glue stay behind

- **Proprietary storage.** Aurora, AlloyDB, and HorizonDB run Postgres on storage you can't take with you; only logical paths leave. AlloyDB Omni, the same engine licensed to run on your own machines, is the partial exception. Neon's storage is open source, but running it yourself is a project of its own.
- **Proprietary features.** EDB's Oracle-compatible syntax, AlloyDB's columnar engine for analytics, Aurora's Babelfish (SQL Server wire compatibility), and the sharded tables of Aurora Limitless Database. Fine to use; count each as work to redo.
- **Platform services.** Supabase Auth, Storage, and generated APIs; Heroku's add-on wiring; Nile's per-tenant storage model. The data comes out with `pg_dump`. The application around it does not.
- **Provider extensions, roles, and glue.** One-service extensions, `rds_superuser`-style roles, dashboards, IAM authentication, private networking, backup policies. Not code, but weeks of work.

### Keep the door open

- **Stay on community PostgreSQL features** unless a proprietary one is worth its exit cost, and write that decision down.
- **Run a logical export on a schedule** to storage the provider account can't delete, and restore it somewhere else once a quarter. That's your exit rehearsal and your [tested backup](/books/eight-kilobytes/backups#test-your-restores) in one.
- **Check publisher support now**: subscribe a plain PostgreSQL server you control to staging.
- **Keep major versions current.** Each major gets five years: 13 reached end of life on November 13, 2025 and 14 follows on November 12, 2026 ([PostgreSQL](https://www.postgresql.org/support/versioning/)). Extended support charges you for staying behind, and a current version widens where you can move.

## The short version

- Above the buffer cache, every provider runs the same PostgreSQL. Below it, where the pages live dominates benchmarks once the working set exceeds RAM.
- Network storage costs a round trip per miss but survives the host; local NVMe is far faster per read and dies with it.
- Treat every published benchmark as vendor-reported, and run your own on your queries.
- Before moving for faster storage, prove reads dominate: working set against RAM, `IO` waits, `shared_blks_read`. Fix queries and buy RAM first.
- Hyperscalers win on breadth, specialists on Postgres focus, serverless platforms on idle cost and branching. Find each one's documented limit before you commit.
- Choose by data size, write volume, extension and superuser needs, compliance, region, RPO and RTO, and budget model, in that order.
- Plan the exit up front: logical replication out, a scheduled `pg_dump` you control, and a written list of every proprietary feature you depend on.
