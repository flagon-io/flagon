Generic is the real risk, and it's the one that never trips an alarm. Nobody complains about beige. Nobody remembers it either. Most companies drift toward the average because the average is defensible, then wonder why nothing they make lands. We would rather be distinctly ourselves and occasionally wrong than inoffensive and invisible.

## Sameness is the risk you don't notice

Blending in feels safe because no single choice looks reckless. You pick the same stack, the same layout, the same rounded-corner tone as everyone else, and each decision is individually reasonable. Added up, they make something nobody has a reason to care about. The slow slide into generic is how most products die, quietly, of no cause anyone can point to.

So we treat sameness as a cost, not a default. When a choice is up for grabs, "what does everyone else do" is a data point, not the answer.

## Conviction over consensus

Have opinions and be willing to be disliked for them. A product that a smaller group loves beats one that a larger group finds fine, because fine doesn't get talked about, defended, or missed when it's gone. We'd rather make a call, say why, and own it than sand every edge off until there's nothing left to disagree with.

This is not an excuse to be contrarian for sport. The goal isn't to argue; it's to actually decide something, in a direction, with a reason. See [operating principles](/handbook/operating-principles) and [how decisions get made](/handbook/decisions) for how we hold that line without turning every choice into a debate.

## Taste is part of the job

Character isn't a coat of paint you add at the end. It comes from caring about the parts most people skip: the empty state, the error message, the wording of a button, the weight of a line. Taste is just a lot of small decisions made by someone who cared, and it's one of the few things that's genuinely hard to copy. We hire for it, we protect it, and we give people the room to apply it. Craft is how personality earns its place, rather than being announced.

## Distinct, not a costume

There's a difference between a point of view and a gimmick. Quirk for the sake of attention wears off fast and usually covers for a lack of substance. We're not interested in being weird to seem interesting; we're interested in being clear about what we believe and letting that show. The mark of it isn't a wacky office or a mascot. It's that our choices are legibly ours, made on purpose, and they hold up when you ask why.

Distinctiveness that comes from conviction lasts. Distinctiveness that comes from wanting to be noticed does not.

## Where this shows up

In the product, in the writing, in this handbook, in the [brand](/brand). Anywhere we're tempted to reach for the default, that's the moment to ask whether the default is actually right or just easy. Usually it's worth the extra thought to make something that could only have come from us. It's part of why we build [in the open](/handbook/values): if the work is going to be visible, it may as well be worth looking at.
