A company that can't explain how it makes money is usually hiding something, so here it is plainly. Today, Flagon makes money building software for clients. Tomorrow, we want most of it to come from products of our own. The two are connected on purpose, and both follow the same rule: the price is public, it's the same for everyone, and you know it before you start.

## Client work pays the bills today

We take on a small number of client projects at a time, through four offers: Product studio for software people sign in to and use, Business websites, Fractional CTO, and a Care plan to keep it all running. Every one of those has a [published rate](/work#rates). There's no quote that depends on how much we think you can afford, and no "contact us for pricing." Custom work needs a conversation about scope, but never about the rate.

This isn't a detour from making products. Plenty of good software companies started this way, building for other people until they understood a problem well enough to build the thing that solves it. Client work keeps the company alive without outside money, keeps our skills sharp on real problems, and shows us, again and again, what's missing from the tools everyone is stuck with. How we choose that work and run it is in [client work](/handbook/client-work).

## Products are the long game

When a product is ready, it charges the people who use it, for what they use, at a price close to what it costs us to run, with our markup stated out loud. Each product sets and publishes its own pricing on its own site, because the team that owns it knows its costs and its users best. What they share is the principle: honest, close to cost, the markup in plain sight, no per-seat pricing, no bill shock. The philosophy behind that gets its own page in [priced close to cost, on purpose](/handbook/priced-close-to-cost).

Products are self-serve. You can start, grow, and leave without talking to a salesperson. No annual contract you have to sign before you can try the thing, and no price that changes based on who's asking.

## Revenue from people glad to pay

The healthy kind of revenue, from a client or a customer, comes from people who'd be a little sad if we vanished: they got real value and think the price was obviously fair. That's who the business is built around. A client who'd hire us again and a customer who'd tell a friend are the same kind of win, and neither needs a trap to keep them.

<Callout type="brand">
  We'd rather make less per customer and keep far more of them, glad, than squeeze the ones we have until they resent it. A business that only holds customers because leaving hurts has already lost the argument on the merits.
</Callout>

## Sustainable on purpose

This model only works if it keeps the lights on, and we take that seriously; the discipline behind it is in [staying alive](/handbook/staying-alive). But the point of being sustainable isn't to maximize what we extract. It's to never be so desperate that we're tempted to. A company that isn't worried about next month doesn't need to reach for the dark patterns, or take a client it shouldn't, and that freedom is worth more to us than the margin we'd gain by giving it up.
