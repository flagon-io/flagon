Flagon makes several products, so the biggest product decision we make isn't a feature. It's whether a product should exist at all. Deciding that is mostly deciding what not to start. Here's the rule we hold ourselves to, so the decision isn't remade from scratch every time a good-sounding idea shows up. The day-to-day version, the way a product team sets its own priorities once it exists, is [deciding what to build](/handbook/deciding-what-to-build).

## What earns a product a start

A new product costs more than its code. It takes a team's full attention, a name, a site, docs, support, and a promise to the people who come to rely on it. So an idea has to clear a few bars before it becomes one.

- **A real problem for developers.** We build for [developers](/handbook/who-we-build-for), and the problem has to be one we've felt or watched people feel, not one we've reasoned our way into from a market slide.
- **A point of view.** If the best we can offer is a slightly cheaper copy of something that already works, we pass. We start a product when we think the thing should be done differently, and can say how in a sentence.
- **Something we'd be proud of in ten years.** [The long game](/handbook/values#the-long-game) applies to products too. A quick hit we'd quietly abandon is not worth the trust it would cost.
- **Pricing that can be honest.** If the only way to make it pay is per-seat pricing, a hidden meter, or lock-in, it fails [how we make money](/handbook/how-we-make-money) before it starts.
- **Someone to own it.** A product without a small team that owns it end to end isn't a product, it's a liability. We don't start more than we can give a real home.

g1t is the worked example. Cloudflare asked, in its "Build the Next-Gen Git Platform" competition, what a git forge looks like when most of the people using it are agents. That was a real problem developers are already running into, and we had a clear view of the answer: a forge to move to, not one more tool to bolt onto GitHub. Keep everything people expect from GitHub, and let any number of agents work the same issue at once, each checked in a clean sandbox before anything merges. It cleared the bars, so it became a product.

## Then let it be itself

Once a product exists, it's its own thing. It gets its own name and brand (not "Flagon something"), its own site, docs, roadmap, changelog and pricing, and its own repo and stack, chosen by the team that owns it. We let each product do what it's going to do, within the company's values and quality bar. The company doesn't hand down a feature list; the team that's closest to the users decides.

Inside a product, the rule is build up, not sideways. Get the foundation of the thing right first, then add what makes it more useful. Don't wander into unrelated features because a competitor has them, because a pile of features bolted onto the side just makes a worse version of five other tools.

## When we stop one

Not every product will work, and pretending otherwise is how companies end up carrying things nobody believes in. We stop a product when it stops clearing the same bars that started it: the problem turned out not to be real, someone else solved it better and we have nothing different to add, it can't pay for itself honestly, or we can no longer give it a team that cares.

Stopping is a decision like any other, so it's made in the open and written down with the reason. And it's done the way we'd want to be treated as users: with plenty of notice, a working way to take your data with you, and no quiet decay where the thing is technically up but nobody is home. Ending a product well is part of building it well.

<Callout type="note" title="Starting is cheap, stopping is the test">
  It's easy to say yes to a new idea. The discipline is in saying no to most of
  them, and in being honest when one we said yes to isn't working.
</Callout>

## We decide in the open

None of this happens behind a curtain. What a product is weighing and has committed to lives on its own public roadmap, the reasoning for starting or stopping a product gets written down the same way we [make every other decision](/handbook/decisions), and the products themselves are on [our products page](/products). If we change our minds, you'll see why.
