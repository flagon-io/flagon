Flagon is a company that makes products, and each product is its own thing. It has its own name, its own site and docs, its own pricing, its own roadmap, and a small team that owns it end to end. [g1t](https://g1t.sh) is the first. There is no central product organization deciding what every product does next. We let each product do what it's going to do, within the company's [values](/handbook/values) and quality bar.

So product at Flagon is small, and it's meant to be. Inside each product team there may be someone whose job is to keep the team close to the users it serves and honest about how the product is actually performing, so that the right decisions get made by the people doing the work. If you're used to product as a command function, this will feel different. It's supposed to.

## The one idea to take away

Product managers here bring clarity and context. They don't own roadmaps or dictate what to build next. Their job is to make sure the team deeply understands its users and its product's performance, so that good decisions happen naturally instead of being ordered from above.

<Callout type="brand">
  A PM's success isn't measured in features shipped. It's measured in how well the team understands who it's building for and whether what it built worked.
</Callout>

Everything else in this section is a consequence of that idea.

## What's shared and what isn't

Each product team decides its own direction, keeps its own public roadmap and changelog on its own site, and sets its own prices. The company doesn't pick features for a product or rank one product's backlog against another's.

What is shared is the way of working on these pages: stay close to users, know how the product is doing, decide as a team, ship small, and price honestly. Those hold for every product, whatever it is and whoever it's for. Whether to start a new product at all is a company question, covered in [which products to build](/handbook/which-products-to-build).

## Why it works this way

The alternative, a product manager who decides and an engineer who executes, quietly wastes the best thing about the people we hire. Our engineers can [do product themselves](/handbook/product-engineering): propose what to build, talk to users, own the outcome. If a PM inserts themselves as the person who decides, they become a bottleneck and they turn thinking engineers into ticket-takers. So the PM's role is deliberately not to decide. It's to make sure everyone has what they need to decide well.

This is the same instinct as the rest of the company: [small teams](/handbook/how-were-structured), real ownership, as little process as we can get away with. A product team is the clearest case of it.

## Who this is for

Anyone working on one of our products. Product managers, obviously, where a team has one. But engineers should read it too, because so much of it is shared work. The discovery calls, the metrics reviews, the calls about what's worth building: those are things a PM makes easy and an engineer stays just as responsible for. Product isn't a wall you throw ideas over. It's a habit the whole team keeps.
